A tutorial · self-organized criticality as a model of research

The Theory Sandpile

A 1987 model of sand grains and avalanches, built with Agents.jl and re-read one line at a time: a grid cell is a theory, a grain is a scientist, and an avalanche is a research cascade — one more worker setting off a chain of theories taken up in sequence.

Below is that exact rule set, running live in your browser: a 22×22 field, one new scientist arriving at a time, redistributing to neighboring theories whenever a theory saturates. No two runs look alike — but given enough time, every run drifts to the same crowding, independent of where it started. That drift, with nothing tuning it, is the entire subject of this page.

Switch the rule for how a new scientist picks a topic and watch the texture of the avalanches change — that comparison is the model's main result, explained in full further down.

01 · What self-organized criticality is

An ordinary critical point is something you have to tune to. Heat a magnet and it's ordered below its critical temperature, disordered above — and exactly at that one temperature, fluctuations stop having a characteristic size. That's a single, precisely-tuned point in the space of possible temperatures.

Self-organized criticality (Bak, Tang & Wiesenfeld, 1987) is the claim that certain systems reach that same scale-free state without anyone tuning anything — because the critical state is where the dynamics settle on their own, not a special value of a parameter. Four ingredients make it happen:

  1. Slow drive — stress is added gradually (one grain, one scientist, at a time).
  2. A threshold — nothing happens locally until a cell holds too much, then it relaxes and pushes the excess outward.
  3. Fast relaxation — the cascade this triggers finishes completely before the next grain arrives. This separation of timescales is what makes "an avalanche" a well-defined, countable thing.
  4. Conservation with a boundary leak — nothing is created or destroyed inside the system; it only ever leaves at the edges.

That last ingredient does the real work. Below the critical density, input piles up faster than it can leave, so the density rises. Above it, avalanches become so easy to trigger that too much leaves at once, so the density falls. The fixed point of that feedback loop is exact marginal stability — a system perpetually one grain away from a cascade of any size.

The signature this leaves behind is a power law with no characteristic scale:

scaling form
P(s) ~ s−τ · G(s / LD)

— avalanche sizes distributed across every scale up to the system size L, with a cutoff that has to move with L. A power law measured at one grid size alone proves very little; that is exactly why the finding in §05 is checked at several system sizes rather than just one.

A caution worth carrying into §05: SOC is a narrower claim than "I found a power law." Lognormal distributions, preferential attachment, and plain bad binning all produce something that looks power-law-shaped on a bad plot. Clauset, Shalizi & Newman (2009) is the standard fix, and it is the method this page's own numbers were checked against.

02 · Theories as a sandpile

The Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld rule, unmodified: a square grid, each cell holding some number of grains. Add a grain to a random cell. Any cell holding four or more sheds one grain to each of its four neighbors. Grains that fall off the edge are gone. Repeat the shedding until every cell is stable, then add the next grain.

Nothing about that needs sand. Read the same five rules as a model of who works on what:

sandpilethis model
cella theory
grid edgetwo theories close enough that work on one carries into the other
graina scientist currently working on that theory
zc = 4how many scientists a theory absorbs before it's considered worked out
topplingthe theory saturates; its newest arrivals spill into adjacent, bridging theories
avalanchea research cascade — one more worker sets off a chain of theories taken up in sequence
boundary lossscientists who leave the field

Two additions the sand never needed:

Development is irreversible. A count of every arrival a theory has ever had, not just its current occupancy — unlike sand height, this number never goes down. A theory is "developed" the moment that count passes zero.

Priority credit. The first few scientists to arrive at a theory bank decayk credit each; everyone after them banks nothing. This is what gives a theory a real capacity: not a physical limit, but the fact that staying past the first few arrivals stops paying. When a theory does saturate, it's the latest arrivals who move on — the earliest keep their claim and stay, which is exactly BTW's four-grains-out arithmetic with a reason attached to which four.

03 · Five ways to choose a topic

Everything above is fixed. The one open question is how a new scientist picks where to start — and that single choice turns out to decide almost everything about the avalanches that follow.

control

:uniform

Starts anywhere, no comparison at all. This is plain BTW — the control every other rule is judged against.

your rule

:avoid_crowded

Samples a handful of theories, joins the least-worked one. Rational — credit is already gone on a crowded theory — but as §05 shows, this is the rule that breaks scale-free behavior.

rich get richer

:matthew

The opposite: joins the most crowded of the sampled theories. Hot topics attract more people, Merton's Matthew effect built directly into the entry rule.

adjacent possible

:frontier

Looks for an undeveloped theory sitting next to a developed one — working the known edge outward, one bridging theory at a time.

the finding

:novelty

A continuous dial, novelty_weight ∈ [0,1], from crowd-avoidance up to an active preference for undeveloped theories, plus a small chance a saturating theory spawns new ones beside it. This is the rule §05 is about.

The live simulation above is tracking exactly these numbers for every mode — try switching from uniform to avoid crowds and watch largest so far climb while the cascades themselves become rarer: fewer avalanches, each one worse. That trade is the whole story of §05, before the numbers get involved.

05 · The novelty-seeking transition

Sweep novelty_weight from 0 to 1 — crowd-avoidance at one end, active novelty-seeking plus a little new-theory creation at the other — and fit the avalanche-size tail at each point with a truncated power law (s−τ·e−s/smax), checked against a lognormal alternative the way Clauset, Shalizi & Newman (2009) prescribe. 11 runs, grid size 24, 40,000 measured steps each, after a 15,000-step warm-up discarded as transient.

The transition is a step, not a slope. Below novelty_weight = 0.5, τ averages 0.91 ± 0.02; at or above it, τ drops to 0.52 ± 0.03 — and mean avalanche size roughly 2.5×s, from the 265–285 range up to 660–705. In both regimes the truncated power law beats the lognormal alternative decisively (log-likelihood ratio R > 20, p < 10−100), so this isn't "critical vs. not critical" in the crude sense — it's a real, large shift in how critical, hinging on a single incentive.

Read the other way: a population of scientists who are individually and rationally avoiding crowded theories does not produce smooth, scale-free research cascades. It produces rarer, more extreme ones — an avalanche-size distribution with a visible bump at large s instead of a clean cutoff, the signature of a characteristic scale creeping back in. What pulls the field back toward scale-free behavior is specifically the preference for novel theories, not mere crowd-avoidance — the two look similar in a one-line description and are opposites in what they do to the field.

06 · What this is, and isn't, good for asking

This is a mechanism model: a fixed 2D lattice, a conserved population, no domain content in what a "theory" actually contains. It's built to answer comparative and structural questions, not to forecast a real field.

  • “

    Mechanism sensitivity — which features of the credit rule actually set the exponent? Is the transition at novelty_weight ≈ 0.5 meaningful, or an artifact of this particular scoring function?

  • “

    A falsifiable empirical claim — real research bursts (citation cascades, a sudden crowd into a subfield) should show a truncated-power-law size distribution under novelty-seeking, and a bump-shaped, non-scale-free one under pure crowd-avoidance. That's checkable against bibliometric data.

  • “

    Does the transition survive a realistic topology? — theory space isn't a 2D grid. Rerun on a small-world or scale-free graph (GraphSpace in place of GridSpace) and see whether it washes out.

  • “

    Heterogeneous populations — what happens with a mixture of strategies in the same field at once, instead of one archetype at a time?

  • “

    Is critical actually good? — reframe from "is it critical" to "is there a novelty_weight that maximizes distinct theories developed per scientist-step," closer to what the welfare side of this literature actually argues about.

Not a good fit: predicting a specific field's trajectory, dating a specific discovery, or treating the credit numbers as real citation counts. The model has structure, not domain content.

07 · Running it yourself

The reference implementation is Agents.jl, cross-checked against a dependency-free Python port and analyzed with the powerlaw package. Three entry points:

Julia — the model itself

terminal
julia --project=. -e 'using Pkg; Pkg.instantiate()'
julia --project=. run_demo.jl

Python — statistical analysis and the figures above

terminal
pip install numpy pandas matplotlib powerlaw
python3 analyze.py

A video of the field evolving

terminal
python3 make_video.py :novelty 0.7

A native Agents.jl/Makie version (make_video.jl) ships alongside it, producing a true MP4 via Makie's bundled FFMPEG_jll rather than the GIF fallback above — written and reviewed, not yet run in an environment with a working Julia install.

08 · Why Python for the analysis

Checked directly rather than assumed: this project's environment has no R and no Stata installed, only Python with numpy/pandas/matplotlib/powerlaw available through pip. That's not really the deciding factor, though — R's poweRlaw package is just as rigorous an implementation of the same Clauset–Shalizi–Newman estimator. What decided it was the pipeline: the cross-check simulation is already Python, so simulate → fit → plot → animate runs in one process, one language, nothing shelled out.
